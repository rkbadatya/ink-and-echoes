I wasn't the same as I am today.

I used to be the sweetest son my parents had. My elder brother admired me. Mom used to say they had three sons — one engineer, one doctor, and one professor. The elder brother fulfilled part of that dream. Me and my younger brother were still writing our chapters.

I feared biology. Or perhaps I disliked biology teachers — I never knew which. I only knew I wanted it removed from my syllabus.

And the result is what I am.

People once felt proud when their son or daughter got into engineering college. There was ambition in it — money, status, a future that looked solid from the outside. Now you sometimes feel embarrassed saying you are doing engineering. Do not even mention IT or Computer Science. When I chose CSE, elders showered compliments on me. Dad had wished for civil. I had a special interest in computers — graphical design, especially. Now my younger brother does better at that than I do.

---

Mom always praised me for my deeds. I obeyed. I did not fight. I completed whatever task was given. That is why I was the dearest among all. She scolded me sometimes; I loved her anyway.

I was closest to one cousin. We played together, studied together, read together, ate together. I was happy with the life I had. My maternal family felt like one great family. I was never attached to my paternal side. It is obvious — you prefer the part of the family that loves and cares for you more. I waited every summer for the holiday when all of us — me, my big brother, my younger brother, mom, and dad — could go to grandfather's house.

School was different.

I was scared of going until I was six. I enjoyed reading books before they were taught in class. I was not exceptional in primary school, though I stood first for three years — first and second standard never count; everyone passes.

I respected teachers, but fear lived behind that respect. Even now I cannot stand properly before my teachers. Two of them were my favorites. One is in God's world. The other lives happily in her married life.

I had three or four friends. Some liked me; some grew jealous. Anyone would, if you took their position. I visited jungles, trees, open fields. I loved cricket. Even in the hot noon I persuaded my younger brother to play. I preferred bowling. If you ever saw my batting, you could not stop laughing. I am still weak at batting.

In fourth class, a quiz competition was held by the human welfare club. I stood first. A senior — my science teacher's elder son — came second. The prize was two hundred rupees. Dad and I cycled to a nearby block, an hour's journey. We left around four-thirty in the morning so we could reach before the six o'clock exam. That was how Dad raised us.

In fifth class I received a scholarship — four hundred and eighty rupees over two years, twenty rupees at a time. Small money. Large pride.

We had to participate in competitions. Attend classes regularly. Skipping exams was a dream we were not allowed to have. No matter what you did, do your best. Dad never forced me to stand first. But he was happy when I did. Sometimes I surprised them.

I won a compass box for coming first in a debate on Independence Day. I always prepared, but preparation was never enough — I had to add something different. Walking in the rally felt like being proud to be someone.

I felt, in those moments, that I would save people one day. I was patriotic. We also received chocolate and biscuits for participating. I was never after candy. I did not need it.

---

After changing schools I was nervous, but familiar faces were everywhere. I joined upper primary school two kilometres from home. Changing houses was a habit. We never stayed in one rented place more than three years. By my sixth class we were in our fourth rented house — by now, our seventh.

Sixth and seventh class transformed me.

The teachers were familiar. One was Dad's friend. His wife was a relative — an aunt who loves me as her son even now. Two teachers felt like elder sisters. I had nothing to worry about. Aunt's class — she taught math and Oriya — sat in the middle of the room. She solved problems and made us solve them. Only for her I scored ninety-eight and ninety-nine in the sixth and seventh final exams.

One history teacher was my favorite. You know what liking means for a sixth-standard boy.

There was a competitor — Dad's friend's daughter. I was not close to her. My younger brother was. I still do not understand how he does these things. I was, and remain, formal with girls. I cannot go zigzag. I come straight to the point, which some people do not find friendly.

There I was inspired to sing. I recited songs inside myself. One day my aunt-teacher heard me and told Dad to let me rehearse for Diwali. I practiced at home. Mom encouraged me. When she sang bhajans in the evening, I sat beside her.

On Diwali it happened. I won five hundred rupees.

The way I was going felt superb. Prizes for quiz and song followed. Seventh board exams arrived. We were well prepared. During exams I could never find my own answer sheets — they were always with someone else, passed hand to hand, because the hall had little restriction.

For God's grace, I stood seventh at the district level. I did not fully understand what it meant to be in the top ten. Dad was really happy. I was the only student from my block. Alok Sahu and Madhumita Meher from SVM Phulbani were on the list too. I knew Madhumita because her mother was Dad's friend.

---

Meanwhile, my big brother got second class in the tenth board. Dad was upset and tense.

He was transferred to Kantamal. We moved to Phulbani. Big brother joined government college there. I was in eighth. My younger brother in sixth. Wherever I went, debate followed. Here too I found friends and competitors. I was treated well because their best student lagged thirty marks behind me.

We were three close friends — playing together, going to school together, doing everything together. My younger brother was friendlier with my friends than I was. I preferred the company of my big brother's friends.

I joined Diamond Tutorial. Satish sir, Ratha sir, Srinivas sir — I can never forget what they did for me. I bet no one could argue English grammar with Srinivas sir. Satish sir was actually a professor who chose tuition anyway. Suddenly I became the most wanted student in school and coaching. Then came the load of debate, quiz, and competition. Oh God.

Scout group entered my life. I attended two camps in eighth and ninth. In eighth I went with Dad — he was scoutmaster at his school. I made a project on safety in the jungle. Third in adventure activity. Debate failed. I expected that. I enjoyed it anyway.

At school, Gopinath sir was my favorite. Only for him I scored seven hundred and thirty in the tenth board.

There was a pretty girl in tuition. I liked her. I understand now how we do foolish things in childhood so we can laugh at ourselves later. I wonder why I kept connecting with girls when I did not wish to. That is why I preferred joining a boys' high school.

Satish sir inspired me. He spoke about how seniors studied, how they carried themselves, how English-medium students behaved. In ninth class only five boys remained in tuition. The rest went their own way. We stayed at night with Satish and Manu sir. They guided us in study and life. When the current failed, we scared each other — barking like dogs, making loud sounds when the sirs were away.

We celebrated Ganesh Puja and Saraswati Puja with devotion. We went on a trip in ninth class with our tuition teachers. On Teachers' Day, my friends, senior brothers, and sisters decorated the centre. Our plus-two lecturers were invited. I went home to arrange a gift, forgot to change my clothes, and returned in the same ones. Another debate competition followed. No one forced me. I could not control myself. I was habituated by then.

The guests were a math lecturer and a physics professor — both friends of my Bada Mamu from when Grandpa lived in G.Udayagiri. They had not seen me in seven years. They knew only my big brother. All three of us brothers studied at the same tuition.

That day I won first prize again. That day they came to know me. I was happy. I still have the photos in my album. Those teachers cannot be compared with any professor I have seen or will see. But this time I got no response from the parents' side for the gift. That hurt more.

---

To bad luck, or something like it, my big brother failed in plus-two.

Guess what happened inside Dad and Mom. Still, they remained quiet.

I wish I could understand them — their feelings, their silence. My respect for them rose. They did not dwell on what had happened. They thought about what to do next. Mom often scolds us. Dad, never.

Now I understand why parents are God. I often think of touching their feet in prayer at least once a day. I am still not able to do it. I am such a loser.

Sometimes I feel disgusted being part of this family. Sometimes I feel proud. I wonder what I would have done in their place, and that thought alone makes me emotional.

I do not know how to love family properly. But I miss them when I am away.

I was never ashamed of being my big brother's younger sibling. He saved me twice in childhood. He always saves me. He will save me in the future too. I have that confidence.

I wasn't like what I am today.

That boy obeyed, competed, sang, debated, stood seventh in the district, and believed — without irony — that he might save people someday.

Engineering took the place biology refused.

Rent took the place of permanence.

Distance took the place of summer holidays at grandfather's house.

But somewhere inside, that boy is still waiting — compass box in hand, cycling behind his father at four-thirty in the morning, certain that the rally leads somewhere worth reaching.
