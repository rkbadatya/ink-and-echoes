I did not expect this kind of friendship.

I had had friends before — people who liked me, who spent time with me, who knew my name and kept it. But they related to me on the surface. They did not ask what was underneath. She did. The first time she chatted with me, she found the loneliness inside me before I had said anything that should have revealed it. I had no way to hide it. She had a way of tracking what people were not saying.

She was like a sister in the truest sense of that word — not because we were similar, but because she saw me clearly and did not use it against me. She was frank, open-minded, genuinely interested in my life and habits. I was conservative, mostly quiet, poor at initiating anything. She had experienced something I was going through now, years before I had. That gave her a patience with me I had not encountered before.

She addressed my unresolved crush in a fraction of a conversation — not dismissing it, but placing it into context the way older siblings place younger siblings' storms into context. *This is real. You are not imagining it. But here is where you are making it harder than it needs to be.* I felt better. I understood the real world a little more.

"How many girl friends do you have?"

"None."

She said I was someone every girl dreams of. I could not agree, but I could not entirely dismiss it either. She said it the way someone says something they genuinely believe, not to make you feel better.

She noticed when I stood silently among unknown people and asked me to join them. Small things. The kind of support you only recognise after someone stops providing it.

---

Then came December — and she started changing me faster than I had agreed to.

She guided me. I accepted. The change was productive — attitude, expression, how I carried myself in rooms. But the pace was unsteady. I was becoming something faster than I understood. Final semester. Leaving everyone soon. A long unfinished story with Natasha. Maybe all of it together made me reach for change the way drowning people reach for anything solid.

She noticed the speed. *You are moving too fast,* she said. *It unbalances things.* She was right. What looks like growth from inside can look like someone becoming unrecognisable from outside.

She is not romantic in this story. She was never that. She was something rarer — the person who showed me where I was going wrong when everyone else was too polite or too distant to say anything. The person who offered friendship first and waited for me to grow into deserving it.

The fear I had was not of falling for her. It was simpler and stranger: the fear of becoming dependent on someone who could see me clearly. What happens when you get used to being understood? What do you do with all the rooms in your life where no one is bothering to look?

**That was the fear that made me talk with her less.**

Not because I was protecting myself from her. Because I was afraid of what I would become if she stopped.

That was the price of someone who stayed — not romance, not rescue, only presence sharp enough to show me what I had been missing, and steady enough to make me afraid of needing it too much.
