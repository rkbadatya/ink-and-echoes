I will be famous.

That was the thought I carried into third semester — partly genuine ambition, partly something smaller and more embarrassing: the hope that if I became someone worth noticing, she might notice me. She did not know I existed. I had started liking her in the diffuse, consuming way that asks nothing back because asking anything back would require speaking to her, and I could not do that. So instead I translated the feeling into a plan. I would improve. I would become notable. Visibility, I had decided, was a kind of courage I could attempt without needing to open my mouth.

Then came the result.

It was a horrible semester. Overconfidence and distraction and a slow accumulation of missed lectures I had told myself I would catch up on and never did. When the numbers came back I did not look at them for two days. Not because I was afraid of them, but because I was afraid of what they said about me — that I had been given every resource and had still found a way to waste it. That I was the kind of person who let things slip not through bad luck but through insufficient seriousness. That she — had she ever looked past a glance — would have had every reason to look away again.

I sank that week. Properly, not just metaphorically. I stopped eating at the right times. I stayed in my room too long. The thoughts that came were dark and I did not know what to do with them except wait for them to pass, which they did, slowly, because somewhere underneath the failure was still the shape of a promise I had not broken yet — my mother's voice, my father's early mornings, the fifteen kilometres he cycled so I could reach exam centres on time. You do not give up when those things are still standing behind you.

I came back. Slower this time, more careful, as if I had finally understood the difference between trying and performing. I started improving — fourth semester, fifth, sixth. I gained a kind of local standing in my branch: not fame exactly, but recognisability. Juniors knew the name. Friends appeared in other departments. Swati beat me to the topper position in fourth semester by 0.04 — the first time I had felt jealous of another student, and then almost immediately felt something shift into respect. Dev took it the next time. I was not jealous then. I was genuinely glad, which told me the jealousy the first time had not been about her at all, but about some version of myself I had been holding accountable.

Our eyes still met sometimes on the bus. To her it was casual. To me it continued to be everything.

---

During those dark weeks I kept returning to the same images.

My father cycling fifteen kilometres so I could reach exam centres. Waking before dawn not for himself but for me. Taking insults from others on my behalf without ever making me feel the weight of it. I had never thanked him directly — not in words, not in the way people thank each other for large things. In our house large things were simply done and not discussed.

My mother quietly reserving money from her own expenses for my college fees. Feeding me from her portion when there was not enough for everyone. Suppressing whatever she had wanted for herself so that my future had room to fit. Hiding pain so I would not be disturbed by it.

I had not become a doctor. That had been her dream and I had not fulfilled it — not through rebellion, but through a series of small choices that accumulated into a direction she had not asked for. I carry that guilt the way you carry something you know is too heavy to put down.

What I miss more than anything is the boy I was before ambition started changing the texture of things. The one who enjoyed his friends without calculating the value of being seen with them. Who feared his father in the good way — the way that comes from love, not anger. Who cried when his mother was sad. I became more efficient somewhere along the way, and I am not sure what I exchanged for it.

I want to be simple. That is all, at the core of it. A good friend. A good son. A good student. Not famous — fame changes how people treat you, and I have seen enough of it at the small scale to know I do not want it at any larger one. I want someone to tell me it is enough, whatever I have managed to become.

---

Study became priority again — every hour directed with a precision I had not previously managed. Results improved. On paper I was happy.

In practice I was still missing something I had never had to begin with.

She was not there to share the happiness. She did not know there was happiness to share. The whole recovery, at its hidden centre, had been built around the idea that if I became better, the distance might close — not dramatically, not through a declaration, but through proximity, through being the kind of person she might eventually stand near without the universe making it strange. I had no strategy beyond becoming someone worth the accidental occasion.

By final year everyone had moved on to jobs and interviews and the practical arithmetic of what happens after college. I was still carrying this unfinished thing I had started without a conversation. I did not want to leave without having tried. I had been crushed before by my own negligence. This time I wanted the chapter to close on my terms, not on my failures.

The plan, as I had mapped it in my head, was simply: crush, then friendship, then something more deliberate. That was the sequence. That was the path I had drawn.

Nobody else knew I was walking it. Nobody could help, and I had not asked, and there was a specific loneliness to that — not the loneliness of being without people, but the loneliness of carrying something real in a room full of people who cannot see it.

The weight of numbers had nearly broken me.

Recovery did not make me light.

It only taught me how to carry the weight and keep walking.
