I have never had a best friend.

When someone asks, I do not know what to say. I never wished for one when I could have many good friends instead — fights and support, opinions and mass work, the noise of people who disagree and still return.

But if college gave me anything permanent, it gave me ADARS.

---

**Shreyash** opened the hand of friendship first. Without that, I might have remained ignorant of college life longer than I should have. I am grateful for the phone call that changed things — though I can forget what I receive from friends, I keep what matters safe for the days when we cannot be near.

He may be self-concerned, though never selfish. What I lacked — the ability to cope instantly with new things — he had in abundance. He creates words for thoughts, not new alphabetical inventions but new ways of seeing. He does not care who read how much. He cares how much he knows about what he can know. Sometimes he seems arrogant, dominating. But there are soft points in him too. The question is how to reach them.

He leads. He rarely speaks of his past, which distresses me. I feel like getting angry at him most of the time. Once I asked what he was selfish about. He said eating. I never got the chance to test that answer.

**Amay** I liked from the first day — polite, thorough, smiling in a way that made conversation easy. When he gets the stick, he masters every spell. He describes things in such detail that I sometimes grow bored. Why read so much about one thing? He prefers hostel life. Like Ankur and, in part, Dev, he scores better alone. Shreyash believes in group study, but only when there is more than two days left.

He is good at talking. At pursuing. At telling life stories I enjoy listening to silently. Sometimes I feel angry at him for reasons I cannot explain. He should understand better.

**Dev** is soft toward me — brotherly in a way that sparks something familiar. Sometimes he is out of this world; sometimes full of joy. His way of listening to old songs feels like my elder brother sitting nearby. His selflessness is what I have always wanted.

**Ankur** is joy in a name. His smile liquefies bad mood. He prefers hugging to handshakes, and I like that. He keeps a beard for reasons unknown. He is mysterious — the most mysterious man I know after my father. I liked how he treated me. I learned basics of friendship from him. When help was needed, he was the one who stepped forward. I felt proud that we still had friends like that.

When Shreyash, Amay, and Ankur enter an argument, Dev and I become silent listeners. They do not stop until someone surrenders — Shreyash's practicality against Amay's theories, Ankur carrying more than both. I enjoy the talk-fighting without joining it.

This is what people call the group of ADARS — Amay, Dev, Ankur, Ronak, Shreyash. Each letter from a first name.

---

My friends did not know about my crush or my past. I never let them. I hated when people appended my name to someone else's for amusement.

Once, standing outside the Communication Lab, DKD sir said I was looking decent. I thought I might impress him. The grand viva spoiled that hope.

In Biswal sir's class, Shreyash and I sat on the front bench near the entry gate. Seniors roamed outside as always. Shreyash looked out once, twice, five times. Sir noticed. He came forward, threw Shreyash's notebook outside, and ordered him out. Shreyash could not attend class that day.

In the DAA viva, Amay asked us for the recursive expansion of merge sort from inside the chamber. We were about to help when he went back in. Sir entered. I turned. I was afraid. I do not remember exactly what he said, only that I decided never to cheat again.

These were small scenes in a larger friendship — lab grades, coaching timings, arguments about study methods, the ordinary violence of engineering education.

I wish I could be each of them in my life, at least once.

Please do not compare or take it personally. Even when I write about someone, they are always more than what my experience captured.

ADARS was not a brotherhood forged in drama. It was built in tuition queues, failed vivas, standing outside labs, and the slow recognition that I was no longer alone in a city that had not asked for my story.

That was enough — for then.
