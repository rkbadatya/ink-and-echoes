There are things you spend days rehearsing in your head — conversations you will start, gestures you will make, words you will finally say out loud. You plan every detail until the scene becomes so vivid that you mistake the rehearsal for the real thing.

Then the moment arrives.

The room is different from what you imagined. The faces wear expressions you did not account for. The confidence you carried in your mind turns thin the instant it meets air. You hesitate. You adjust. You shrink the plan from its original grandeur into something safer, smaller, barely recognizable.

I had imagined a hundred percent. I executed fifty. I received ten.

That is the mathematics of cowardice dressed as caution. You tell yourself you will try again, that this was only a rehearsal for the real attempt. But somewhere inside, you know — that was the attempt. The one and only. And you walked away from the checkpoint just before the destination, because staying would have required a version of yourself you could not summon.

The strange part is this: the personality you hate for its hesitation is the same one that carried you through every other storm. You cannot reform it without destroying something that kept you alive.

---

What no one tells you is that falling short does not only hurt you.

You imagine doing something beautiful for people you love — something that will light their faces, make them feel seen, prove that you care as deeply as you do silently. You carry that joy inside yourself for days, certain that when the moment comes, it will be enough.

But joy makes you careless. It blinds you to the small cues — someone's discomfort, someone's need for space, someone's quiet signal that today is not the day. And in your excitement, you say the wrong thing. You push when you should have paused. You become the reason behind the very sadness you wanted to prevent.

The worst guilt in the world is watching someone you love pretend to be fine because you are the cause of their pain. You cannot fix it — your presence only reminds them of the wound. The only thing left to offer is distance, and the fragile hope that time will do what your words cannot.

When you are a mystery, people find you interesting. When you become an open book, they lose the page they were on. And when they leave — not with anger, but with quiet indifference — you realize that wanting someone in your life was never enough to keep them there.

But you wait anyway. Because believing they will return is the only version of the story you can survive.
